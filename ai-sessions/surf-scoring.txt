  2. Curves and gates

  All numbers are my own call unless a source is named.

  - Height [0.3,0] [0.6,0.5] [1.0,1] [2.0,1] [3.0,0]
    - Below 0.3 m it's flat. At 3.0 m or more it's too big.
    - I picked 1–2 m as the sweet spot because the app is for general users, not
      experts.
    - The model's height is "significant height" (roughly the average of the
      biggest third of waves). The faces you actually see on the beach are often
      bigger, especially with long periods. That's partly why the danger gate
      sits at 3 m and not higher.
  - Period [5,0] [8,0.4] [12,1]
    - Breakpoints follow the usual surf-forecast convention: under about 8 s is
      weak, choppy wind swell, 10–12 s is good, 13 s and up is strong
      groundswell.
    - No gate here: a short period makes the waves poor, not dangerous.
  - Wind [11,1] [19,0.7] [28,0.35] [39,0]
    - Points come from the Beaufort scale: up to 11 km/h is "light" (fairly
      glassy water), 12–19 is "gentle" (some whitecaps), 39 km/h is force 6
      (whitecaps everywhere).
    - The gate is at 39 km/h or more, from any direction. Strong onshore wind
      wrecks the waves, and strong offshore wind makes it hard to paddle into
      waves and can push you out to sea.
  - Thunderstorm: ×0.75, the same as outdoor. Lightning is worse in the water,
    but daily weather_code is the worst hour of the day, so a gate would wipe
    out a whole day for a short storm. The reasoning is the same as in your
    outdoor decision.

  The flat and too-big gates match their curve endpoints, and so does wind,
  which follows your rule. I left out a gusts gate because the 39 km/h sustained
  gate already kicks in long before 93 km/h gusts would.

  3. Wind without direction

  Treat any wind as bad wind, which assumes the worst case (onshore). What you
  lose:
  - Light offshore wind is actually the best condition, because it holds the
    waves up so they break cleanly. The formula scores it lower than flat calm.
  - 15–30 km/h is where you can't tell the difference. Offshore at that speed
    can be great, while onshore is junk. Both end up with the same middle score.
  - Swell direction against the beach's orientation is also lost: a big swell
    may simply not reach a beach that faces the other way.
  - The daily max overstates wind. Surfers often go out early, when it's usually
    calmer before the afternoon sea breeze.

  This is why wind gets the lowest weight: the signal is real, but we can only
  read half of it.

  4. Weights: height 45%, period 30%, wind 25%

  - Height is the biggest because without waves there's nothing to surf.
  - Period is second because it decides how good the waves are.
  - Wind is last because of section 3. A surfer would probably weight wind as
    heavily as period, but only if we knew its direction.

  4. Thunderstorm penalty

  I'd pick ×0.5, still a penalty and not a gate.

  - The danger is handled the same way as on land. You get out of the water and
    wait. The NWS 30-minute rule (weather.gov/safety/lightning-sports) applies
    to both, and its "no place outside is safe" covers the water too. I couldn't
    load NWS's water-specific page (it returned HTTP 500), so I'm not citing
    it.
  - What's different is the share of the day lost. A surf session is about 2–3
    hours, not an 8-hour sightseeing day. Losing 1–2 hours to a storm costs
    roughly half the surf day, so ×0.5 instead of ×0.75. That's my call.
  - Why not a gate: the same reason as outdoor. Daily weather_code is the worst
    hour of the day, so a gate would wipe out a whole day for one short storm.

  Known limitation, as you asked

  Height and period are scored separately, while wave energy depends on height²
  × period. So a small swell with a long period (Lisbon on the 19th: 0.5 m at
  13.8 s) scores lower than a surfer would rate it. Not fixed, recorded as a
  limitation.
