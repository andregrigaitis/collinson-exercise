⏺ Short answer: Open-Meteo has no idea there's a ski resort near your city. It
  forecasts for the coordinates you send, at the elevation you send or the one
  it looks up. A fixed elevation only adjusts the temperature calculation. It
  doesn't create mountains or snowpack.

  How Open-Meteo handles elevation

  - Default: It looks up the ground height at your lat/lon in a 90 m elevation
    dataset. It then adjusts the model's temperature for the difference between
    that height and the model grid cell's average height (statistical
    downscaling). The elevation field in the response tells you which height it
    used.
  - &elevation=<metres>: Overrides that lookup. The docs describe it as a way to
    "correctly match mountain peaks", so this is its intended use.
  - &elevation=nan: Turns downscaling off. You get the raw grid-cell average.

  What a fixed elevation actually changes

  - Mostly temperature, and whatever depends on it (freezing level, whether
    precipitation falls as rain or snow, apparent temperature).
  - It does not change the terrain the weather model uses. Models run on grids
    of roughly 2–25 km depending on the model, so mountains are smoothed out.
    Precipitation, wind, cloud and especially snow_depth are grid-cell values.
    snow_depth in particular is coarse and often far too low for alpine terrain.
  - It isn't checked against reality. Send elevation=2000 for Lisbon and you'll
    get a believable cold forecast for a mountain that doesn't exist. A fixed
    elevation would make every city look skiable on cold days, which is the
    opposite of what your ranking needs.

  The city-centre problem

  Geocoding gives you the town centre, which sits in the valley. Chamonix is
  about 1,035 m, but its ski areas run from about 1,000 m to 3,800 m. So:
  - The default elevation will understate how cold and snowy the slopes are.
  - But the default at least gives the real local height, so coastal and lowland
    cities correctly come out bad for skiing.

  Options, from simplest to best

  1. Use the default elevation and score from the data. Base the score on recent
     snowfall_sum, snow_depth and temperature. It's honest and simple, but it
     will underrate valley towns next to resorts.
  2. Look for nearby mountains (my recommendation). Build a small grid of points
     within about 20–30 km of the city (say 3×3 or 5×5) and send them all in
     one call to Open-Meteo's Elevation API (/v1/elevation), which accepts many
     coordinates at once. Take the highest point, or flag the city as "no
     mountain terrain" if the gap from the city is small, say under 500 m. Then
     request the forecast at that point. This doesn't use the fixed-elevation
     parameter at all, so the elevation can't be invented. The forecast endpoint
     also accepts many coordinates in one request, so it costs about one extra
     call.
  3. Use a list of resort locations, such as OpenStreetMap's
     landuse=winter_sports areas or a ski-area dataset. It's the most accurate,
     but it's a real data dependency and likely too much for this exercise.

  For an exercise I'd do option 2, or option 1 with option 2 noted as the next
  step. Whichever you pick, record the assumption in DECISIONS.md: Open-Meteo
  only adjusts temperature for elevation, it doesn't know about terrain, so the
  skiing score depends on the proxy point you choose.

  What's actually going on

  1. The rain/snow split follows the adjusted temperature. In the same grid cell
     (45.90), moving from 2500 m to 2800 m turns most of the rain into snow
     (5.8 → 12.7 cm), and snow depth goes from 0.21 m to 0.29 m. This is the
     rain-vs-snow effect I described in my first answer. I then contradicted it
     in my second answer by saying snowfall and snow depth don't change.
  2. The grid cell itself changes. The coordinates in the response move between
     45.90, 45.92 and 45.94 depending on the elevation you ask for. It looks
     like Open-Meteo picks a neighbouring cell whose height is closer to the one
     requested. That's why 1800 m shows no snow at all while 2500 m does.
  3. The total amount stays roughly the same. Precipitation stays at about 24–27
     mm whatever the elevation. Elevation changes what form it falls in, not
     how much falls. The effect also levels off: there's still 4.1 mm of rain at
     4000 m with a mean of −8 °C. So it's a correction applied after the model
     run, not a new physical simulation.
  4. One thing I can't explain: elevation=nan reports 1261 m but gives 12.88 cm
     of snow and a 3.6 °C maximum. Those are colder than a 1261 m point should
     be. I wouldn't rely on nan.

  What this means for your design

  - A fixed elevation is worse than I said. It doesn't just make cities look
    cold, it creates snowfall and snow depth wherever cold and precipitation
    coincide. Lisbon at 2000 m could show snow during a winter storm.
  - The proxy elevation you choose strongly affects the result. Moving from 2500
    m to 2800 m roughly doubled snowfall here. Whatever you do (nearby-terrain
    sampling, a cap, a percentile), it's a parameter that drives the score, so
    record it in DECISIONS.md.
  - The overall recommendation still holds. Sample the real terrain nearby, and
    don't ask for an elevation the city doesn't have nearby.
